\begin{abstract}
Collision avoidance in urban driving is difficult when other road users are hidden by occluders or lie outside a limited sensor field of view. This paper reports an occlusion-aware deep reinforcement learning system implemented in the Nocturne simulator. The policy uses a class-weighted ego-centric occupancy grid, stacked observations, ego and goal features, and explicit occlusion summaries. Control is learned with a double dueling deep Q-network using noisy layers, three-step returns, prioritized replay, and the Muon optimizer. The available checkpoint was evaluated deterministically for 1,500 episodes. It reached the goal in 95.33\% of episodes and collided in 5.13\%, with mean return $109.99\pm48.84$. On the 1,089 episodes for which trajectory statistics were recorded, average displacement error was $2.24\pm2.13$ m, final displacement error was $6.40\pm6.20$ m, and jerk was $10.27\pm4.08$ m/s$^3$. These results establish that the trained policy can frequently complete the sampled scenarios, but they do not establish an advantage over a baseline: only one seed and one evaluated checkpoint are available, 411 episodes terminate after one step, and seven episodes are marked as both goal-reaching and colliding. We therefore present the study as a reproducible empirical report and identify the additional controls required for a defensible safety claim.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
autonomous driving, collision avoidance, deep reinforcement learning, partial observability, occlusion, Nocturne, double DQN

\section{Introduction}
Autonomous driving policies must act before every relevant hazard is fully visible. Parked vehicles, roadside structures, traffic, and a restricted sensor cone can hide road users until only a narrow margin remains for braking or steering. This makes collision avoidance a partially observable decision problem rather than a purely reactive control problem. Classical approaches address this uncertainty with reachable sets, hypothetical hidden agents, or explicit belief-state planning \cite{orzechowski2018tackling,bouton2018scalable}. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) offers an alternative in which behavior is learned from interaction, but its empirical safety must be measured carefully \cite{garcia2015comprehensive,kiran2021deep}.

We study collision avoidance in Nocturne, a simulator that replays logged urban-driving scenarios and supports efficient multi-agent experimentation \cite{vinitsky2022nocturne}. The implementation combines a spatial occupancy representation with occlusion-risk features and time-to-zero (TTZ) conflict information. A value-based controller selects from discrete throttle--steering combinations and is trained with a scalable actor--learner loop.

The intended research question is: \emph{Can an occlusion-aware value-based policy reach logged goals while maintaining a low collision frequency under partial observation?} The supplied artifacts support a limited but concrete answer. A single deterministic evaluation contains 1,500 episodes and a complete training configuration. Accordingly, this paper makes three bounded contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item it specifies a reproducible occlusion-aware observation, reward, and double dueling DQN training configuration;
  \item it reports episode-level safety, task-completion, trajectory, and smoothness statistics with confidence intervals; and
  \item it audits the evaluation record, separating measured results from comparisons and causal claims that remain unsupported.
\end{itemize}

This distinction is important. The current evidence supports describing the behavior of one checkpoint. It does not support a state-of-the-art claim, a statistically controlled comparison with a non-occlusion agent, or a claim that explicit occlusion features caused the observed performance.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Driving Simulation and Logged Scenarios}
CARLA and SUMO are widely used for synthetic autonomous-driving experiments \cite{dosovitskiy2017carla,lopez2018sumo}. Nocturne instead replays real-world motion scenarios at high throughput and was designed for learning interactive driving policies \cite{vinitsky2022nocturne}. Its source data derive from large-scale motion records such as the Waymo Open Motion Dataset \cite{ettinger2021waymo}. Logged replay increases behavioral realism, although replayed non-ego agents may not respond to the learned ego policy in the same manner as fully interactive agents.

\subsection{Collision Avoidance Under Occlusion}
Prior work has treated occluded intersections as sequential decision problems and demonstrated that DRL can learn useful behavior in such settings \cite{isele2018navigating}. Model-based safety methods reason about hidden occupancy using set-based verification or sensor-occlusion POMDPs \cite{orzechowski2018tackling,bouton2018scalable}. The present system is model-free at decision time: it augments visible-scene features with low-dimensional summaries of occlusion and learns a discrete control policy. This design does not provide formal safety guarantees.

\subsection{Value-Based and Safe Reinforcement Learning}
The agent combines double Q-learning, which reduces maximization bias \cite{van2016deep}, with a dueling value--advantage decomposition \cite{wang2016dueling}. Prioritized replay \cite{schaul2015prioritized}, noisy exploration \cite{fortunato2017noisy}, and multi-step targets are components associated with strong value-based agents such as Rainbow \cite{hessel2018rainbow}. These mechanisms improve learning efficiency but do not by themselves constrain unsafe actions. Constrained objectives and shielding provide stronger safety semantics \cite{achiam2017constrained,alshiekh2018safe}; in contrast, the studied system uses reward penalties and must therefore be assessed empirically.

\section{Problem Formulation}
We model each episode as a partially observable Markov decision process $\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,r,\Omega,O,\gamma\rangle$. The simulator state $s_t\in\mathcal{S}$ contains the ego vehicle, surrounding road users, and map geometry. The policy receives an observation $o_t=O(s_t)$ restricted by visibility and feature construction. It selects a discrete action $a_t\in\mathcal{A}$, receives reward $r_t$, and seeks to maximize
\begin{equation}
J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_t\right], \qquad \gamma=0.99.
\end{equation}
The configured horizon is 80 steps at a simulator interval of 0.1 s. Episodes may end after goal achievement or collision; the evaluation record also permits both flags to occur in one episode, a semantic ambiguity discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

The action space is the Cartesian product of three throttle values $\{-1,0,2\}$ and five steering values $\{-0.7,-0.2,0,0.2,0.7\}$, giving 15 discrete actions. This compact action space is suitable for Q-learning but can induce abrupt control changes.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Observation Representation}
The configuration uses a $20\times10$ ego-centric occupancy grid spanning 40 m forward, 5 m backward, and 10 m laterally. Vehicles, vulnerable road users, and road edges receive weights 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Three consecutive frames are stacked. Ego-state and goal-relative features are included, while the subscriber restricts perception using a 2.1 rad view angle and 80 m view distance.

Occlusion processing is enabled in both the subscriber and occupancy-grid configuration. The implementation is designed to add summaries such as occluded view fraction, blocker proximity, blocker density, and occlusion pressure. These features provide the value network with evidence about missing visibility rather than falsely exposing hidden-agent state. The supplied evaluation artifact, however, does not contain per-step feature traces, so feature utilization cannot be verified from the result JSON alone.

\subsection{Reward}
The configured shaping reward can be summarized as
\begin{align}
r_t={}&100\,\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{goal}}-100\,\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{collision}}-50\,\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{offroad}} \\
&-0.1+2(d_{t-1}-d_t)+0.3h_t+0.5z_t,
\end{align}
where $d_t$ is distance to the goal, $h_t$ is a heading-alignment term, and $z_t$ is a TTZ-derived term with a 3 s safety threshold. The repository configuration also contains simulator-level terminal settings with a collision penalty of 1,000 and goal bonus of 100. Because both configuration blocks are present, the exact reward path should be logged explicitly in future runs to avoid ambiguity.

\subsection{Double Dueling DQN}
The action value is decomposed into value and centered advantage terms,
\begin{equation}
Q(o,a)=V(o)+A(o,a)-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{a'}A(o,a').
\end{equation}
For a sampled transition, the three-step double-Q target is
\begin{equation}
y_t=R_t^{(3)}+\gamma^3(1-d_t)Q_{\bar\theta}\!\left(o_{t+3},\arg\max_a Q_{\theta}(o_{t+3},a)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ and $\bar\theta$ denote online and target parameters. Prioritized replay samples transitions according to their temporal-difference errors, with exponent $\alpha=0.6$ and importance-sampling coefficient annealed from $\beta=0.4$.

The network has four hidden layers of 256 units and uses noisy layers and a dueling head. The replay capacity is $10^6$, batch size is 512, target update interval is 1,000 learner steps, and maximum gradient norm is 10. The learning rate is $3\times10^{-4}$. Muon is enabled for compatible hidden matrices; it is an implementation choice rather than an experimentally isolated contribution.

\subsection{Training System}
The supplied run used four workers and two environments per worker in synchronous vector-environment mode. Experience collection and learning are decoupled through a bounded learner queue; the configured training frequency is 512 transitions. TensorFloat-32, cuDNN benchmarking, and PyTorch compilation are enabled. The nominal training budget is 500,000 episodes, while the evaluated checkpoint name indicates 400,000 episodes. Only seed 42 is represented in the supplied configuration.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\subsection{Evaluation Data}
The result file identifies method \texttt{ddqn}, checkpoint \texttt{ddqn\_ep400000.pth}, and 1,500 evaluation episodes. The agent is described in the accompanying history as evaluated greedily with exploration disabled. Every episode records return, length, goal and collision flags, and the number of vehicles. Trajectory and comfort metrics are present for 1,089 episodes; TTZ-to-pedestrian is present for 362 episodes.

All episodes report 50 vehicles. Consequently, the data do not support density-stratified conclusions. No non-occlusion policy, behavior-cloning policy, classical controller, alternative optimizer, or additional random seed is included.

\subsection{Metrics and Uncertainty}
Goal and collision rates are empirical proportions. We report normal-approximation 95\% confidence intervals for these rates. For a continuous metric $x$ with $n$ observations, we report sample mean $\bar{x}$, sample standard deviation $s$, and the approximate 95\% interval of the mean $\bar{x}\pm1.96s/\sqrt{n}$. These intervals quantify finite evaluation-episode uncertainty for one trained policy; they do not represent variation across training seeds.

Average displacement error (ADE) and final displacement error (FDE) compare the evaluated trajectory with the logged reference trajectory. Jerk measures control smoothness in m/s$^3$. The artifact also reports a metric named \texttt{smoothness}; because its precise formula is not included in the result file, we preserve its name but avoid interpreting its absolute scale.

The trajectory subset yields an ADE of 2.24 m and FDE of 6.40 m. FDE has high dispersion, indicating substantial scenario-to-scenario variation. Mean jerk of 10.27 m/s$^3$ is also high for comfortable passenger motion and is consistent with the use of discrete acceleration and steering commands. These observations motivate a continuous-control or action-smoothing comparison, but such a comparison is not available in the current record.

\subsection{Outcome Consistency Audit}
The goal and collision events are not mutually exclusive: seven episodes have both flags set. No episode has neither flag. Moreover, 411 episodes, or 27.4\%, terminate after one step. These properties may be valid consequences of scenario initialization and event ordering, but they can also inflate the apparent completion rate if an ego vehicle begins inside the goal tolerance. At minimum, future evaluation should report exclusive terminal categories in a fixed priority order and separately identify initial-goal episodes.

Trajectory metrics are absent from the same 411 one-step episodes, which explains why ADE, FDE, jerk, smoothness, and vehicle TTZ each have $n=1{,}089$. Pedestrian TTZ has still lower support. Comparing these conditional metrics directly with methods that use a different missingness rule would be misleading.

\subsection{What the Results Do and Do Not Show}
The results show that the evaluated checkpoint frequently triggers the configured goal criterion on the sampled scenarios while collisions are recorded less often. They also show nontrivial trajectory deviation and abruptness. They do not isolate the effect of occlusion-aware features because no otherwise identical checkpoint without those four features is present. Likewise, the narrow episode-level confidence intervals do not establish training stability: a multi-seed interval could be much wider.

\section{Discussion}
The combination of spatial encoding, explicit visibility cues, and TTZ features is a plausible inductive bias for partially observed driving. A purely visible occupancy grid tells the policy what is present but not why regions are empty. Occlusion summaries can distinguish genuinely clear space from space hidden by blockers. TTZ features, meanwhile, compress relative position and velocity into a conflict-oriented statistic. A learned value function can use these signals to trade progress against risk.

The evaluation suggests a useful policy was learned, but the present evidence should be treated as a pilot result. A strong final study requires a paired factorial design: with versus without explicit occlusion features; full versus restricted visibility; Muon versus AdamW; and DDQN versus behavior cloning or a rule-based controller. Each condition should use at least three training seeds and an identical held-out scenario manifest. Evaluation should include bootstrap intervals over scenarios and seed-level summaries, with paired tests where policies see the same episodes.

Safety analysis should also stratify by occlusion severity, number and class of nearby agents, minimum TTZ, intersection type, and episode length. A penalty sweep could trace a goal-rate--collision-rate Pareto frontier rather than selecting one reward weight implicitly. Finally, action smoothing or continuous control should be tested because the current jerk values indicate that task completion and comfort are not aligned automatically.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
\textbf{Single policy and seed:} the artifact contains one checkpoint associated with seed 42, so training variance is unknown. \textbf{No comparator:} no baseline or ablation result is available; causal and superiority claims are therefore excluded. \textbf{Terminal-label overlap:} seven episodes record both success and collision, making the rates nonexclusive. \textbf{Initial-state effects:} 411 one-step episodes may represent starts already satisfying the goal criterion. \textbf{Metric missingness:} trajectory and TTZ statistics are computed on subsets, not all episodes. \textbf{Fixed density:} every episode reports 50 vehicles, preventing density analysis. \textbf{Reward ambiguity:} two collision-penalty settings appear in the configuration hierarchy. \textbf{External validity:} results come from one scenario source and simulator and do not imply real-vehicle safety.

\section{Reproducibility Checklist}
A reproducible rerun should preserve the checkpoint, complete resolved configuration, source commit, scenario manifest, seed, evaluation command, and raw per-episode JSON. Exploration must remain disabled during evaluation. The evaluator should add explicit \texttt{timeout}, \texttt{offroad}, \texttt{initial\_goal}, and exclusive \texttt{terminal\_reason} fields. It should also store the resolved observation dimension and whether occlusion features are enabled, preventing checkpoint/configuration mismatches. For statistical reporting, training should be repeated over multiple seeds and all policies should be evaluated on the same ordered scenario list.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented an occlusion-aware double dueling DQN system for collision avoidance in replayed urban-driving scenarios and analyzed the supplied 1,500-episode evaluation. The checkpoint records a 95.33\% goal rate and 5.13\% collision rate, with ADE 2.24 m and FDE 6.40 m on episodes carrying trajectory statistics. The episode-level evidence is promising but insufficient for a comparative safety claim because it contains one seed, no baseline, overlapping terminal labels, and many one-step episodes. The most important next step is not a stronger claim but a controlled multi-seed ablation campaign with exclusive outcome accounting and scenario-matched comparisons.

\begin{abstract}
The novelty of this work is collision avoidance learned on a simulator that
replays a real-world driving dataset, under a ray-cast sensor model, with a
risk-aware reward. The agent has to choose an action when the hazard is not
fully visible: a vehicle can sit behind a truck, inside the field of view,
and still be absent from the observation. Most
reinforcement learning (RL) results for driving assume the opposite --
fully observed, synthetic traffic. We study risk-aware collision avoidance
under partial observability, training deep RL agents on real-world driving
scenarios from the Waymo Open Motion Dataset replayed in the Nocturne
simulator, whose ray-cast visibility model restricts perception to what a
physical sensor could see: hidden vehicles remain hidden. The observation
has two levels the policy may use, visible state and sensor-derived
occlusion geometry. Hidden state and future trajectories are withheld from
the policy. Our agent
observes an ego-centric occupancy grid and analytic time-to-zero (TTZ)
conflict features, both filtered to visible objects, together with
\emph{occlusion-risk summaries} that expose how much of the view cone is
blocked and by what; a TTZ-barrier shaped reward penalizes risky closing
behavior before a collision becomes unavoidable. A double dueling DQN is
trained at scale with an asynchronous actor--learner architecture that runs
unchanged from a single workstation to a Ray cluster. We additionally compare late-training dynamics for PPO and risk-aware
R-MAPPO on the same occlusion-aware setup at episodes 400k--430k. In the
completed DDQN held-out evaluation on 1{,}500 episodes, the agent reaches the
goal in 95.33\% of episodes (95\% Wilson CI: 94.15--96.29\%) with a 5.13\%
collision rate (95\% Wilson CI: 4.13--6.37\%). On the 1{,}089 episodes with
non-degenerate trajectory traces, mean ADE is $2.24 \pm 2.13$\,m, mean FDE
is $6.40 \pm 6.20$\,m, and mean absolute jerk is
$10.27 \pm 4.08$\,m/s$^3$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Autonomous vehicles must make safety-critical decisions with incomplete
information. A parked truck hides a crossing pedestrian; a vehicle in the
adjacent lane masks a merging car; a limited field of view leaves a
fast-approaching agent undetected until seconds before conflict. In such
\emph{occluded} and \emph{sensor-limited} situations the danger is by
definition not yet visible when the avoiding maneuver must begin -- which is
precisely why occlusion is treated as a first-class hazard in the planning
literature~\citep{orzechowski2018tackling, bouton2018scalable}. Yet the
majority of reinforcement learning (RL) results for driving are obtained
under full observability in synthetic traffic, where every agent's state is
available to the policy and the hardest part of the problem is assumed away.

This paper studies collision avoidance as a problem of decision-making
under partial observability. We train and evaluate deep RL agents
on real-world driving scenarios from the Waymo Open Motion
Dataset~\citep{ettinger2021waymo} replayed in the Nocturne
simulator~\citep{vinitsky2022nocturne}, whose C++ ray-casting engine
restricts the agent's perception to objects a physical sensor could actually
see: vehicles behind blockers are invisible, and the view cone is limited in
angle and range. The agent must reach a goal position extracted from the
recorded human trajectory while avoiding collisions with road users that
replay their logged behavior -- confronting it with unprotected turns,
merges, jaywalking pedestrians, and occluded conflicts drawn from real
roads rather than procedurally generated traffic.

While imitation learning from human demonstrations produces strong driving
policies~\citep{bansal2018chauffeurnet, codevilla2019exploring}, it inherits
the biases of the demonstration distribution and offers no mechanism for
recovering from states human drivers rarely visit -- precisely the states in
which collisions occur. RL offers a complementary route, but introduces its
own tension: a reward that pays for progress and penalizes collisions
induces an explicit \emph{safety--efficiency trade-off}, in which the agent
may learn to accept a nonzero collision probability in exchange for reaching
goals faster. Rather than treating this trade-off as a nuisance, we measure
and characterize it directly.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Collision avoidance on replayed real logs, under a sensor
  model.} We train on Waymo scenarios in Nocturne. The observation has three
  levels of information, and the policy is restricted to the first two.
  Level~1 is the visible state: an ego-centric occupancy grid and analytic
  time-to-zero (TTZ) features over objects the ray cast returns. Level~2 is
  sensor-derived uncertainty: the occluded fraction of the view cone, the
  distance to the nearest blocker, blocker density, and occlusion pressure.
  Level~3 is hidden ground truth, including objects behind blockers and the
  logged future trajectories. Level~3 is a privileged training reward and an
  evaluation label. The policy input is levels 1 and 2
  (Section~\ref{sec:observation}).
  \item \textbf{Risk-aware reward shaping.} We combine sparse terminal
  signals with a TTZ-barrier penalty that activates only when a conflict is
  imminent, providing a dense safety gradient before a collision becomes
  unavoidable (Section~\ref{sec:reward}). The collision penalty $R_c$ remains
  an explicit safety--efficiency knob; we report one operating point and leave
  a full Pareto sweep for follow-on work.
  \item \textbf{Stratified, statistically grounded evaluation.} We evaluate a
  trained DDQN checkpoint on 1{,}500 held-out episodes with Wilson intervals
  for binary rates, and stratify failures by vehicle TTZ imminence and
  episode length, alongside ADE/FDE and jerk-based smoothness
  (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:results}). We further report late
  training returns for PPO and R-MAPPO as on-policy references.
  \item \textbf{A scalable training system.} A modern value-based learner
  (double dueling DQN with noisy exploration, $n$-step returns, prioritized
  replay, and the Muon optimizer~\citep{jordan2024muon}) is trained with an
  asynchronous actor--learner architecture in the style of Sample
  Factory~\citep{petrenko2020sample} that scales from a laptop to a
  multi-node Ray cluster~\citep{moritz2018ray} without code changes
  (Sections~\ref{sec:agent}--\ref{sec:system}).
\end{itemize}

On a held-out split of Waymo scenarios, the completed single-checkpoint
evaluation covers 1{,}500 episodes and yields a 95.33\% goal rate and 5.13\%
collision rate. The trajectory subset with valid multi-step traces yields
$2.24 \pm 2.13$\,m ADE, $6.40 \pm 6.20$\,m FDE, and
$10.27 \pm 4.08$\,m/s$^3$ mean absolute jerk. Collision mass concentrates in
low-TTZ episodes (15.3\% collision when vehicle TTZ $<1$\,s versus 0\% when
$\mathrm{TTZ}\ge 3$\,s).

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related}
positions our work relative to safe RL, occlusion-aware planning, and
scalable RL systems. Section~\ref{sec:problem} formalizes the task as a
POMDP, and Section~\ref{sec:method} details the observation, reward, agent,
and training system. Section~\ref{sec:setup} defines the metrics and
statistical protocol; Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the held-out DDQN
evaluation, PPO/R-MAPPO training references, and stratified analyses.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Driving simulators from real-world logs.}
CARLA~\citep{dosovitskiy2017carla} and SUMO~\citep{lopez2018sumo} provide
synthetic traffic, while Nocturne~\citep{vinitsky2022nocturne}, Waymax, and
nuPlan replay logged human trajectories, exposing agents to realistic
interaction patterns. We adopt Nocturne for its fast C++ core, ray-cast
partial observability, and native support for the Waymo Open Motion
Dataset~\citep{ettinger2021waymo}.

\paragraph{RL for collision avoidance.}
Value-based and policy-gradient methods have been applied to lane keeping,
merging, and intersection handling~\citep{kiran2021deep, isele2018navigating}.
Occupancy-grid observations are a common mid-level representation that
balances expressiveness and sample efficiency~\citep{fridman2018deeptraffic}.
Time-to-collision-style features have long been used in classical
safety systems~\citep{minderhoud2001extended}; our TTZ features bring this
inductive bias into the observation and the reward.

\paragraph{Safety-critical and risk-aware RL.}
Safe RL formalizes the tension between task performance and constraint
violation, via constrained MDPs~\citep{altman1999constrained,
achiam2017constrained}, risk-sensitive objectives that penalize variance or
tail outcomes of the return~\citep{garcia2015comprehensive}, and shielding
or barrier-style mechanisms that intervene near constraint
boundaries~\citep{alshiekh2018safe}. Our TTZ-barrier penalty is a shaped,
differentiable-in-effect analogue of a control-barrier condition: it applies
increasing pressure as the most imminent conflict approaches, rather than
penalizing only terminal collisions. Unlike hard-constraint methods we do
not guarantee constraint satisfaction; instead we expose the
safety--efficiency trade-off explicitly by reporting the measured operating
point under a fixed penalty coefficient, following the position that in
learned driving stacks the operating point should be a deliberate,
measurable choice.

\paragraph{Driving under occlusion.}
Planning literature treats occlusion by reasoning over phantom agents or
worst-case reachable sets behind blockers~\citep{orzechowski2018tackling},
and POMDP formulations marginalize over hypotheses about unobserved
traffic~\citep{bouton2018scalable}. These approaches hand-design the
occlusion response. In contrast, we give a model-free agent explicit,
low-dimensional occlusion-risk statistics computed from ray-cast visibility
and let it \emph{learn} when caution is warranted, evaluating on real
scenarios whose occlusion patterns arise from logged traffic rather than
constructed worst cases.

\paragraph{Imitation learning baselines.}
Behavior cloning remains a strong supervised baseline for
driving~\citep{bansal2018chauffeurnet, codevilla2019exploring}, but suffers
compounding errors under distribution shift~\citep{ross2011reduction} --
exactly the regime of near-collision states. A matched behavior-cloning evaluation uses the same success definition as
the RL agents: a low collision rate together with a high timeout rate means
the cloned policy stopped instead of reaching the goal.

\paragraph{Value-based deep RL.}
Our agent composes established components: double
Q-learning~\citep{van2016deep}, dueling
architectures~\citep{wang2016dueling}, prioritized experience
replay~\citep{schaul2015prioritized}, noisy networks for
exploration~\citep{fortunato2017noisy}, and multi-step
returns~\citep{hessel2018rainbow}. We additionally adopt the Muon
optimizer~\citep{jordan2024muon}, which orthogonalizes momentum updates for
hidden weight matrices via Newton--Schulz iteration and has shown strong
results in large-scale supervised training; we treat it as a drop-in
optimizer choice and ablate it against AdamW rather than claiming it as a
contribution.

\paragraph{Scalable RL systems.}
High-throughput RL architectures decouple acting from learning:
Ape-X~\citep{horgan2018distributed}, IMPALA~\citep{espeholt2018impala}, and
Sample Factory~\citep{petrenko2020sample} demonstrate that asynchronous
experience collection with centralized (batched) inference dramatically
improves hardware utilization. Our system adapts this recipe to a CPU-heavy
simulator with an off-policy learner, and layers Ray~\citep{moritz2018ray}
on top for multi-node scaling.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

We consider an episodic, partially observed Markov decision process
$(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma, \Omega, O)$. Each episode
instantiates one scenario from the Waymo Open Motion Dataset replayed in
Nocturne at $\Delta t = 0.1$\,s. The state $s \in \mathcal{S}$ comprises the
full simulator state -- poses and velocities of all road users plus static
map geometry. A single ego vehicle is controlled by the policy; all other
road users -- vehicles, pedestrians, and cyclists, the latter two referred
to jointly as vulnerable road users (VRUs) -- replay their logged
trajectories, so the transition kernel $P$ is deterministic given the ego
action. The observation function $O$ is \emph{not} the identity: it first
applies Nocturne's ray-cast visibility model, which removes every object
outside the ego's view cone or occluded behind a nearer object, and then
encodes the surviving objects into the feature vector
$o \in \Omega \subset \R^{613}$ described in Section~\ref{sec:observation}.
Partial observability in our setting is therefore physical (sensor-like
occlusion), not an artifact of feature compression. We separate that
information into three levels. Level~1 is the kinematic state of objects
inside the ray-cast set. Level~2 is geometry computed only from those
visible returns: how much of the view cone they cover, and how close the
nearest cover is. Level~3 is the state the ray cast removed, together with
the future positions stored in the driving log. The learned policy is a
function of levels 1 and 2. Level~3 enters the TTZ reward when that reward
is taken from ground truth, and it supplies evaluation labels such as
whether the nearest object at contact was hidden one second earlier.

The ego receives the
goal position $g \in \R^2$ recorded at the end of the human trajectory and
must reach it within a tolerance while avoiding collisions with other
objects and road edges. An episode is a success only when the goal is
reached and no collision occurs. Termination on the horizon without either
event is a timeout: the agent neither crashed nor completed the drive.
Episodes terminate on goal achievement, collision,
or a horizon of $T_{\max} = 80$ steps (8 seconds).

The action space is a discrete product of throttle and steering levels,
$\mathcal{A} = \{-1.0, 0.0, 2.0\} \times \{-0.7, -0.2, 0.0, 0.2, 0.7\}$,
yielding $|\mathcal{A}| = 15$ actions executed through Nocturne's kinematic
bicycle model.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Observation Design}
\label{sec:observation}

The observation is a flat vector concatenating four groups of features
(Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}).

\paragraph{Ego-centric occupancy grid.}
We rasterize the local scene into a $C \times H \times W$ grid with $C = 3$
channels, $H = 20$ rows, and $W = 10$ columns, covering $40$\,m ahead,
$5$\,m behind, and $\pm 10$\,m laterally in the ego frame. Channel~0 encodes
occupancy strength with class-dependent weights: vehicles ($w_{\text{veh}} =
1.0$), vulnerable road users (pedestrians and cyclists, $w_{\text{vru}} =
2.0$), and road edges ($w_{\text{edge}} = 3.0$); overlapping stamps take the
maximum. Channels 1--2 encode the relative longitudinal and lateral velocity
(normalized by $30$\,m/s) of the \emph{nearest} vehicle occupying each cell,
resolved by a vectorized per-cell minimum-distance reduction. Road-edge
geometry is cached in the world frame once per scenario and re-projected
into the ego frame each step.

\paragraph{Ego and goal features.}
Four ego-state features (heading, speed, heading error to goal, speed error
to the logged target speed, each normalized) and two goal features (distance
to goal normalized by $100$\,m; relative bearing normalized by $\pi$).

\paragraph{Time-to-zero (TTZ) conflict features.}
For every non-ego object $i$ at displacement $\mathbf{d}_i$ from the ego, we
compute the scalar closing speed along the line of sight,
\begin{equation}
  v^{\text{close}}_i \;=\;
  \frac{\mathbf{v}_{\text{ego}} \cdot \mathbf{d}_i}{\lVert \mathbf{d}_i \rVert}
  \;-\;
  \frac{\mathbf{v}_i \cdot \mathbf{d}_i}{\lVert \mathbf{d}_i \rVert},
  \qquad
  \text{TTZ}_i \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \lVert \mathbf{d}_i \rVert \,/\, v^{\text{close}}_i & v^{\text{close}}_i > 0,\\[2pt]
    \text{TTZ}_{\infty} & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\text{TTZ}_{\infty}$ is a finite sentinel (999\,s) and contact
distances below $0.5$\,m map to $\text{TTZ} = 0$. The observation exposes
the minimum TTZ over vehicles, the minimum over pedestrians and cyclists
(both clipped to $20$\,s), and their difference. This is the line-of-sight
range divided by the line-of-sight range rate, the same construction as
time-to-collision along the bearing to each object~\citep{minderhoud2001extended}.
We use it because it is analytic, cheap to compute for every visible object,
and separates vehicles from vulnerable road users. It assumes constant
velocity along the current line of sight, so a vehicle that will turn across
the ego later can still have a large TTZ. The barrier threshold $\tau = 3$\,s
is the onset used in the reward below. The controlled study repeats training
at $\tau \in \{1, 2, 4, 5\}$\,s so the operating point is compared with
nearby values rather than fixed by convention alone.

\paragraph{Occlusion-risk features.}
The source of the visibility set is one call per step to Nocturne's C++
ray caster, \texttt{visible\_objects\_state}, with view distance
$\max(40, 10) + 5 = 45$\,m and view angle $\pi$ (a $180^\circ$ forward
cone). An object is returned only when a ray reaches it. A truck between
the ego and another vehicle removes that vehicle from the set, so the
occupancy grid and the observed TTZ contain the truck and omit the vehicle
behind it.

Each returned object $j$ has range $r_j$, azimuth $\alpha_j$, and a
half-extent $\rho_j = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\ell_j^2 + w_j^2}$ from its length
and width. It subtends the interval
$[\alpha_j - \arctan(\rho_j / r_j),\; \alpha_j + \arctan(\rho_j / r_j)]$,
clipped to the cone $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$. Let $\mathcal{U}$ be the union of
those intervals, $B$ the number of objects with positive angular width, and
$V$ the number of valid returns. The four features are
\begin{align}
f_{\mathrm{frac}} &= |\mathcal{U}| / \pi, \\
f_{\mathrm{near}} &= \min_j r_j \;/\; 45\,\mathrm{m}, \\
f_{\mathrm{den}} &= B / \max(V, 1), \\
f_{\mathrm{press}} &= f_{\mathrm{frac}}\,(1 - f_{\mathrm{near}}).
\end{align}
$f_{\mathrm{frac}}$ is the fraction of the cone covered by visible bodies.
$f_{\mathrm{near}}$ is the nearest of those bodies, scaled by the view
range. $f_{\mathrm{den}}$ is the share of returns that actually cover a
positive angle. $f_{\mathrm{press}}$ is large when a large angle is covered
by something close. If no return covers a positive angle, all four features
are 0. A truck close ahead produces a wide interval, a small
$f_{\mathrm{near}}$, and a high $f_{\mathrm{press}}$. The car behind the
truck is absent from all four numbers. The features describe the blockage
the sensor can measure. They do not name the hidden car.

These four numbers are Level~2. The visibility filter on the grid and on
observed TTZ is Level~1. The revised configuration sets the two switches
independently. The archived checkpoint used a single coupled switch. Under
that switch the C++ occupancy grid stamps every object, because that grid
routine does not take a visibility set, while TTZ and the four summaries
use the ray-cast set. When \texttt{visibility\_filter} is set on its own,
the grid is stamped in Python from the ray-cast set, so hidden objects
leave the grid as well.

With the four summaries the observation has dimension
$3 \times 20 \times 10 + 4 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 613$. Without them it has dimension
$609$. The summaries skip the grid encoder and enter the dueling heads.
Each head is a noisy linear map, which stores a mean matrix and a
standard-deviation matrix, so the parameter difference is
$2 \times 2 \times 256 \times 4 = 4096$ weights. Biases do not grow with
the input width, and the convolutional trunk is shared. Relative to the
rest of the network this increment is negligible; \texttt{scripts/count\_q\_parameters.py}
prints both totals.

\subsection{Reward}
\label{sec:reward}

The per-step reward combines sparse terminal events with dense shaping:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_t \;=\;
\underbrace{R_g \,\mathbb{1}[\text{goal}]}_{\text{goal bonus}}
\;-\; \underbrace{R_c \,\mathbb{1}[\text{collision}]
      \;+\; R_o \,\mathbb{1}[\text{road-edge collision}]}_{\text{safety penalties}}
\;-\; \underbrace{c_s}_{\text{step cost}}
\;+\; \underbrace{c_p \,(d_{t-1} - d_t)}_{\text{progress}}
\;+\; \underbrace{c_h \Big(1 - \tfrac{|\theta_t|}{\pi}\Big)}_{\text{heading}}
\;-\; \underbrace{c_z \,\max\!\big(0,\; \tau - \text{TTZ}^{\min}_t\big)}_{\text{TTZ barrier}},
\end{equation}
where $d_t$ is the distance to goal, $\theta_t$ the heading error,
$\text{TTZ}^{\min}_t$ the minimum TTZ over all object classes, and
$\tau = 3$\,s the safety threshold. We use $R_g = 100$, $R_c = 100$,
$R_o = 50$, $c_s = 0.1$, $c_p = 2.0$, $c_h = 0.3$, $c_z = 0.5$. The shaping
terms are active only on non-terminal steps. The TTZ barrier grows linearly
as the most imminent conflict drops below $\tau$, so the learner receives a
safety gradient while a collision is still avoidable.

In the controlled comparison, $\text{TTZ}^{\min}_t$ is computed from every
object, including objects the ray cast removed. That choice keeps the reward
identical across arms that differ only in the observation. It is a
privileged training signal: the penalty can respond to a hidden closer
before the policy has seen it. The archived checkpoint used the other
setting, in which the barrier sees only the objects that entered the
observation. Setting $c_z = 0$ removes the barrier and leaves the terminal
collision penalty. An 80-step timeout forgoes the goal bonus of $100$ and
pays $0.1$ per step, and it also forgoes the progress term. Those terms are
what penalize a policy that stops in order to avoid collisions. Success,
timeout rate, and mean speed are therefore reported together. A low
collision rate with a high timeout rate is a stalled policy, which we count
as a failure.

\subsection{Agent}
\label{sec:agent}

The agent is a double dueling DQN~\citep{van2016deep, wang2016dueling}. The
grid channels and auxiliary features are encoded and fused by an MLP trunk
(4 hidden layers of width 256), followed by dueling value and advantage
heads. All exploration is state-dependent via noisy linear
layers~\citep{fortunato2017noisy}, annealed jointly with a small
$\epsilon$-greedy floor ($\epsilon: 1.0 \to 0.05$ over $5 \times 10^5$
steps). Targets use $n$-step returns ($n = 3$) with double Q-learning
bootstrapping:
\begin{equation}
  y_t = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \gamma^k r_{t+k}
  + \gamma^n \, Q_{\bar\theta}\!\big(s_{t+n},\,
    \argmax_{a} Q_\theta(s_{t+n}, a)\big).
\end{equation}
Transitions are sampled from a prioritized replay
buffer~\citep{schaul2015prioritized} of $10^6$ transitions ($\alpha = 0.6$,
$\beta: 0.4 \to 1$, sum-tree implementation) with importance-sampling
correction. The target network is synchronized every $10^3$ gradient steps.
Hidden weight matrices are optimized with Muon~\citep{jordan2024muon}
(Newton--Schulz orthogonalized momentum), while embeddings, biases, and
head layers use AdamW; the base learning rate is $3 \times 10^{-4}$ with
$\gamma = 0.99$, batch size 256, and gradient-norm clipping at 10.

\paragraph{Why double DQN, and why a discrete action space.}
The action space has 15 elements, so a Q-network can rank them with one
forward pass. The max operator in standard Q-learning uses the same network
to choose the next action and to score it, which systematically
overestimates values~\citep{van2016deep}. Overestimated values are a poor
fit for collision avoidance: an action that looks safe only because its
value was inflated is exactly the action we want the penalty to suppress.
Double Q-learning selects the next action with the online network and
evaluates it with the target network, which is the bootstrap in the target
above. The same finite action set is what makes that split straightforward.
A continuous actor--critic method would remove the chattering that a
15-way discretization can introduce, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations}
treats that as open. Every arm of the comparison below uses this same
15-action set, so a difference in collisions is attributable to the
observation and the TTZ term. Off-policy replay is also the practical match
for this simulator: stepping Nocturne is the expensive part, and a replay
buffer lets the learner reuse those transitions. The late-training windows
in Section~\ref{sec:algo-compare} are the empirical companion. PPO stays
high, and both risk-aware R-MAPPO runs collapse at episode 430k, while DDQN
is the method with a finished deterministic held-out evaluation.

\subsection{Asynchronous Training System}
\label{sec:system}

Nocturne is CPU-bound: stepping the C++ simulator plus Python-side feature
extraction dominates wall-clock time, while the Q-network is small enough
that per-environment GPU inference wastes the accelerator. We therefore
adopt an asynchronous architecture in the spirit of Sample
Factory~\citep{petrenko2020sample}, with three roles:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Actors.} $N_w$ worker subprocesses each step $N_e$
  environments sequentially ($N_w \times N_e$ environments total; 48 workers
  $\times$ 2 environments in our default configuration). Observations are
  written into per-worker shared-memory buffers (zero-copy transport), each
  worker is pinned to CPU cores, and every environment receives a distinct
  seed to decorrelate experience.
  \item \textbf{Batched inference (main process).} The main process gathers
  ready observations across workers, performs a single batched forward pass
  on the GPU, and scatters actions back through pipes. Inference weights are
  refreshed from the learner every 16 gradient steps.
  \item \textbf{Learner (background process).} A separate process owns the
  replay buffer and performs gradient steps. Transition batches arrive
  through a bounded multiprocessing queue with configurable overload policy
  (block, drop-newest, or drop-oldest); a train-step scheduler enforces a
  fixed replay ratio (one gradient step per 512 ingested environment steps
  after a 5{,}000-step warm-up), preventing both learner starvation and
  runaway off-policyness. The learner periodically publishes a compact
  inference-state snapshot through a latest-value queue.
\end{itemize}

The same training loop supports four execution modes selected by
configuration: \texttt{dummy} (single-process, sequential; for debugging),
\texttt{sync} (lock-step vectorized), \texttt{async} (the architecture
above), and \texttt{ray}, in which actor workers become Ray
actors~\citep{moritz2018ray} and the system scales across a multi-node
cluster with scenario data synchronized from object storage to node-local
disk on first access. Off-policy learning makes this decoupling benign: the
policy lag introduced by asynchronous weight sync is absorbed by the replay
buffer rather than biasing the gradient, in contrast to on-policy
architectures that require importance-weighted
corrections~\citep{espeholt2018impala}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Asynchronous training loop (main process)}
\label{alg:async}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Spawn $N_w$ actor workers ($N_e$ envs each) with shared-memory buffers
\STATE Spawn learner process; wait for initial inference weights
\WHILE{total steps $<$ budget}
  \STATE Collect ready observations from workers (shared memory)
  \STATE $a \leftarrow \argmax_a Q_\theta(s, a)$ \hfill (single batched GPU forward)
  \STATE Dispatch actions to workers; receive $(s, a, r, s', d)$ batches
  \STATE Submit transition batch to learner queue (bounded, overload policy)
  \STATE Poll learner: ingest metrics; load refreshed weights if published
\ENDWHILE
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data.}
Training uses the processed Waymo training directory. The held-out directory
is partitioned by a stable hash of the filename (SHA-256 of the split seed
and the filename, first 8 bytes interpreted as a uniform value). With
\texttt{split\_seed}$=42$ and \texttt{val\_fraction}$=0.5$, each file lands
in validation or test and stays there across seeds and arms. Validation is
for checkpoint selection. Test is the split quoted in the controlled
comparison. The archived 1{,}500-episode evaluation used the held-out
directory without this hash partition; the artifact does not record how
many distinct files were drawn, and that count is not inferred here.
Each episode replays one scenario for up to 80 steps at 10\,Hz; non-ego
road users follow their logged trajectories. Scenarios in which the ego is
removed at goal or collision follow the standard Nocturne configuration.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Success is the fraction of episodes that reach the goal with no collision.
A timeout is an episode that hits the 80-step horizon with neither a goal
nor a collision. Goal rate and collision rate are still reported, and an
episode can carry both labels when the evaluator records both events.
Collision type uses the simulator flag: object--object contact
(\texttt{veh\_veh\_collision}, which covers vehicles and vulnerable road
users, since the engine has a single object--object type) or road-edge
contact. The partner label is the type of the nearest object within 5\,m
when the contact is object--object.

Minimum TTZ is the per-episode minimum of the ground-truth time-to-zero.
On a collision episode we also record ground-truth TTZ one second and two
seconds before the terminal step. The anticipatory lead is the time from
the first 1\,m/s speed drop, inside the two seconds before the minimum-TTZ
step, until that minimum. A positive lead means the agent had already
slowed before the closest approach. The maneuver in that same window is
brake if the speed drops by at least 1\,m/s, lateral if any steering
command has magnitude at least $0.2$\,rad, both, or neither.

Local density is the mean number of vehicles inside 40\,m, the forward
extent of the grid. The archived field \texttt{num\_vehicles} is the count
of vehicles in the whole scenario file and is 50 on every archived episode,
so it cannot support a density curve. Occlusion level is the mean of
$f_{\mathrm{frac}}$ over the episode, binned as $[0,0.1)$, $[0.1,0.3)$,
$[0.3,0.6)$, and $[0.6,1]$. Pressure uses the same episode mean of
$f_{\mathrm{press}}$. A collision is labeled hidden when the nearest object
within 5\,m was outside the ray-cast set 10 steps (1\,s) earlier.

Average speed is reported next to the logged expert speed. ADE, FDE, mean
absolute jerk, and steering-rate magnitude are unchanged. Eval-time
robustness, applied only at test time on a frozen checkpoint, zeros the
four occlusion features with probability $p$, adds Gaussian noise of scale
$\sigma$ to the observation, or shrinks the view cone from $\pi$ to
$2\pi/3$.

\paragraph{Statistical protocol.}
The currently completed evaluation contains 1{,}500 held-out episodes from
one trained DDQN checkpoint (training seed 42). For binary goal and collision
rates we report 95\% Wilson score intervals over episodes. For continuous
trajectory metrics we report the episode-level mean $\pm$ standard
deviation and the number of valid traces. These intervals quantify
evaluation-episode uncertainty, not training-seed uncertainty. Consequently,
we do not perform between-method significance tests on the present single-run
held-out result. PPO and R-MAPPO are compared only on late training-window
returns.

\paragraph{Stratified evaluation.}
Aggregate rates hide where a policy fails. Because every evaluation episode
reports a fixed surrounding-agent count of 50, traffic-density curves are not
identifiable from the artifact. We instead stratify safety metrics along two
recoverable axes: (i) \emph{episode length} (1-step; 2--10; 11--30; 31--50;
51--80 steps), which proxies scenario duration and interaction complexity;
and (ii) \emph{vehicle TTZ imminence} ($<1$\,s, $1$--$2$\,s, $2$--$3$\,s,
$\ge 3$\,s, or missing), isolating performance in near-conflict regimes.

\paragraph{Algorithm comparison.}
We compare three RL families trained to $\sim$400k episodes on the same
occlusion-aware environment: double dueling DQN, PPO, and risk-aware
R-MAPPO (two reported runs). For DDQN we additionally report a
deterministic held-out evaluation over 1{,}500 validation episodes
(Table~\ref{tab:main}). For PPO and R-MAPPO we report the late
1{,}000-episode training windows at episodes 400k, 420k, and 430k,
including mean reward, exploration rate, and terminal goal distance
(\texttt{goal\_dist}).

\paragraph{Controlled comparison.}
The archived runs coupled three choices in one flag: ray-cast filtering of
TTZ, the four occlusion summaries, and a C++ occupancy grid that still
stamped hidden objects. The revised comparison separates them. Every arm
except the oracle uses the ray-cast visibility filter, including on the
occupancy grid. Every arm uses ground-truth TTZ in the reward, or turns
that term off. The arms are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A: visibility filter, no occlusion summaries, $c_z = 0$.
  \item B: visibility filter, occlusion summaries, $c_z = 0$.
  \item C: visibility filter, no occlusion summaries, $c_z = 0.5$.
  \item D: visibility filter, occlusion summaries, $c_z = 0.5$. This is the
  proposed agent.
  \item Oracle: no visibility filter, no occlusion summaries, $c_z = 0.5$.
  Full state is an upper bound, since the policy can see the objects the
  sensor would hide.
\end{itemize}
A and B differ by the four summaries. C and D differ by the same four
summaries, with the barrier present in both. A and C differ by the barrier.
B and D differ by the barrier. The network for A and C has the 609-D
observation; B and D have the 613-D observation; the parameter gap is the
4096 weights above. Seeds 42, 43, and 44 repeat A--D and the oracle.
Thresholds $\tau \in \{1, 2, 4, 5\}$ and scales $c_z \in \{0.25, 1.0\}$
repeat arm D at seed 42. Behavior cloning is evaluated with the same
success, collision, timeout, and speed metrics. The launch script is
\texttt{scripts/launch\_reviewer\_ablation.sh}, and it refuses to start
unless training has been explicitly enabled on the training machine.

\paragraph{Training details.}
The completed DDQN run used synchronous vectorization with four workers and
12 environments per worker (48 parallel environments), a replay buffer of
$10^6$, and the hyperparameters of Section~\ref{sec:agent}; the full
configuration is in Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}. The contiguous late-training
log reaches episode 347{,}000 with 11{,}757{,}888 environment steps and
22{,}955 learner updates at approximately 283 environment frames per second;
additional windows at episodes 400{,}000 and 405{,}000 mark the evaluated
checkpoint region. PPO and R-MAPPO late windows are reported at episodes
400{,}000--430{,}000.

\subsection{Safety--Efficiency Operating Point}
\label{sec:tradeoff}

With $R_c=100$ (and the TTZ barrier at $\tau=3$\,s, $c_z=0.5$), the held-out
operating point is 95.33\% goal rate at 5.13\% collision rate. A multi-value
penalty sweep was not completed in the supplied artifacts, so we do not draw
a Pareto curve. The single measured point already shows that the shaped
reward yields high goal completion with a non-zero residual collision rate,
consistent with an explicit safety--efficiency compromise rather than a
hard constraint.

\subsection{Scaling and Throughput}

The supplied synchronous DDQN run improves from 228 to 283 environment frames
per second in the contiguous late log (439 at the episode-400k window) and
reports zero queue lag and dropped transitions. Matched asynchronous or
multi-node scaling curves were not supplied.

\subsection{Scope of Reported Experiments}

The numbers in Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:curves} are the
archived DDQN run. Component ablations of noisy layers, prioritized replay,
$n$-step returns, and velocity channels are not in that bundle. The
occlusion and reward arms defined in Section~\ref{sec:setup}, the
density and occlusion bins, the hidden-contact breakdown, and the
eval-time dropout, noise, and field-of-view probes are produced by the
revised evaluator and written into Section~\ref{sec:controlled} when the
training machine deposits the JSON files. Within the archived data, the
algorithm comparison covers DDQN (held-out eval + training), PPO
(training), and R-MAPPO (two training runs). Stratified held-out analysis
of that checkpoint uses episode length and vehicle TTZ.

Occlusion and density interact in a way the four summaries only partly
address. $f_{\mathrm{frac}}$ and $f_{\mathrm{press}}$ grow when many nearby
bodies cover the cone, and they saturate once the cone is already full, so
a further hidden vehicle does not change the observation. The bins in
Section~\ref{sec:controlled} separate sparse scenes from scenes with 15 or
more vehicles inside 40\,m, and low occlusion from occlusion above 0.6, and
they report timeouts beside collisions. A rise in collisions at high
density with a flat timeout rate is a perception failure. A rise in
timeouts with a drop in speed is the policy becoming overly cautious. The
barrier scale $c_z$ and the threshold $\tau$ are the two knobs the sweep
uses to move that trade-off. Memory of the occluded region, or an explicit
phantom agent behind the blocker, would be a different observation. This
paper measures the four-summary policy first.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Non-ego road users replay logged trajectories and do not react to the ego,
so the agent cannot learn to exploit (or be robust to) reactive behavior of
others; closed-loop multi-agent training is a natural extension.

\paragraph{Discrete vs.\ continuous control.}
The 15-action set is what makes double Q-learning a direct fit: the online
network can enumerate the argmax, and the target network can score that
single action. The same enumeration is a coarse throttle and steering
quantization. Jerk and steering-rate in Section~\ref{sec:setup} are the
measurements of the resulting chatter. Continuous control (SAC or TD3)
would score actions by an actor instead of an argmax, which removes the
overestimation argument that selected DDQN and changes the exploration
problem. A finer discretization, up to the 630 actions Nocturne already
supports, sits between the two. The controlled comparison holds the
15-action set fixed.

\paragraph{Safety guarantees.}
Our approach is risk-\emph{aware}, not risk-\emph{guaranteed}: the TTZ
barrier shapes behavior away from imminent conflicts but provides no formal
constraint satisfaction, and the residual collision rate -- 5.13\% at the
measured $R_c{=}100$ operating point -- remains above the threshold required
for deployment in safety-critical systems. Combining the learned policy with
a verified shield or control-barrier filter is a natural direction.

\paragraph{Evaluation scope.}
The training log includes a three-way comparison at episodes 400k--430k
(DDQN, PPO, two R-MAPPO runs). Only DDQN has a matching 1{,}500-episode
held-out evaluation with goal/collision rates and trajectory metrics. The
archived JSON stores \texttt{num\_vehicles}$=50$ on every episode, a count of
vehicles in the file. Density in the revised evaluator is the number of
vehicles inside 40\,m, reported with timeouts in the same bins.

\paragraph{Other limitations.}
The occupancy grid discards agent identity and history beyond relative
velocity; our evaluation horizon (8\,s) is short relative to full-route
driving; and results are in a 2D simulator, so transfer to
perception-driven stacks is out of scope.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a risk-aware deep RL system for collision avoidance under
physically grounded partial observability, trained on real-world driving
logs: an observation design that makes occlusion risk explicit through
ray-cast visibility filtering and dedicated occlusion-risk summaries, a
TTZ-barrier shaped reward that provides a dense safety gradient before
conflicts become unavoidable, and a stratified evaluation that localizes
residual risk. In the completed single-checkpoint evaluation, the DDQN agent
attains a 95.33\% goal rate and a 5.13\% collision rate across 1{,}500
held-out episodes, with ADE of $2.24 \pm 2.13$\,m and FDE of
$6.40 \pm 6.20$\,m over the 1{,}089 valid multi-step traces. Collisions
concentrate where vehicle TTZ is below one second (15.3\% collision rate)
and in mid-horizon episodes. Late PPO training returns exceed DDQN
exploration returns, while R-MAPPO exhibits a sharp late collapse in both
reported runs; only DDQN currently has held-out evaluation. Treating the
safety--efficiency compromise as an explicit operating point, together with
log-replay realism and high-throughput off-policy training, is a practical
recipe for studying safety-critical driving behaviors at scale.
